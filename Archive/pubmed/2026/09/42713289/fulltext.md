# Rheological modulation and microstructural densification of hydrogels induced by Choy sum polysaccharides: Dual applications in dysphagia diets and anthocyanin stabilization


## Abstract
  Texture-modified foods facilitate dysphagia care and nutrient supplementation, yet conventional high-molecular-weight polysaccharide-gelatin hydrogels suffer phase separation, sticky texture and aspiration risks. Herein, cold-set composite hydrogels of gelatin and low-molecular-weight Guangdong Choy sum polysaccharides (GCSP, Mw = 71.49 kDa), were constructed to tune swallowing-friendly rheology and stabilize anthocyanin. DLS and FTIR characterizations corroborated strong hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between GCSP and gelatin, forming micron aggregates. SEM observations validated the microstructural densification. GCSP reduced porosity from 78.5% (neat gelatin) to 36.2%, building a dense, thick-walled interpenetrating network. The compact network slowed anthocyanin photodegradation, retaining over 40% after 10-day UV exposure. Moreover, GCSP lowered hardness (250.3–142.5 g) alongside springiness, while preserving high cohesiveness (∼0.85) and desirable thixotropic recovery, mitigating brittleness and excessive stickiness. Swallowing simulations complied with IDDSI Level 4/5 standards. This work elucidates polysaccharide-regulated protein rearrangement, offering a dual-functional matrix for safe elderly dysphagia food with anthocyanin photoprotection


## Introduction
  The global population ageing has led to a substantial rise in the prevalence of dysphagia. This condition greatly elevates the risks of aspiration pneumonia, malnutrition, and dehydration, posing threats to both quality of life and long-term survival in affected individuals (Cichero, 2018; Wang et al., 2025). To ensure safe oral feeding for dysphagia individuals, the development of texture-modified foods (TMFs) has become a vital research focus in clinical nutrition and food engineering (Hadde & Chen, 2021). In accordance with the International Initiative for Standardization of Dysphagia Diet (IDDSI) framework, high-quality TMFs require precisely controllable macroscopic rheological properties (Hassan et al., 2024). Recent advances in food oral processing indicate that TMFs need optimal yield stress and distinct shear-thinning properties to enable smooth pharyngeal transit of food boluses under shear loading (Chen, 2015). Meanwhile, TMFs should possess sufficient structural cohesiveness to prevent premature bolus disintegration prior to swallowing, thereby reducing the risk of airway aspiration and choking complications (Fan et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2025).

  Despite these well-established guidelines, formulating TMFs that simultaneously meet swallowing safety requirements and provide adequate nutritional fortification remains a formidable challenge (Liang et al., 2026). Incorporating bioactive compounds such as plant polyphenols into TMFs is a crucial strategy for nutritional fortification. As potent natural antioxidants, anthocyanins exert favorable anti-inflammatory and anti-ageing physiological effects (Liang et al., 2026). Nevertheless, their practical application is severely limited by the high susceptibility of molecular structures to external environmental stimuli. During food processing and storage, anthocyanins readily undergo hydrolysis and irreversible oxidative degradation, especially under light and thermal treatments (Liang et al., 2024). However, their practical application is severely constrained by high susceptibility to hydrolysis and oxidative degradation, especially upon exposure to light, heat, and oxygen (Lei et al., 2023). Consequently, advanced carrier matrices are urgently needed that can conform to strict rheological criteria for dysphagia diets while simultaneously providing robust physical confinement to stabilize labile micronutrients.

  Biomolecular composite hydrogels featuring tunable viscoelastic networks have emerged as ideal candidate substrates for developing dysphagia-specific TMFs (Jia et al., 2026). Protein-based hydrogels, particularly gelatin, have been extensively investigated as candidate due to their biocompatibility, thermoreversible gelation, and favorable release profiles for encapsulated actives (Ahmad et al., 2024). However, single gelatin networks tend to undergo gel-sol phase transition near human body temperature, and their inadequate thermal stability and mechanical strength cannot support them as independent protective barriers (Dong et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2020). To address these shortcomings, incorporation of polysaccharides to construct protein-polysaccharide composite gels has become a mainstream modification strategy (Abubaker et al., 2026; Bains et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b, Hu et al., 2025). Notably, polysaccharide molecular weight profoundly governs the thermodynamic evolution of composite systems. Numerous studies have demonstrated that high-molecular-weight (HMW) polysaccharides can improve system viscosity, yet easily induce severe macroscopic phase separation caused by strong steric hindrance and depletion flocculation, forming loose and porous networks (Kim et al., 2025). In addition, HMW polysaccharides usually confer gels with excessive extensional viscosity and stringy texture (Dong et al., 2025a; Junaid et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024). Such undesirable rheological properties not only fail to meet IDDSI safety specifications, but also increase the risks of bolus adhesion and pharyngeal residue. These inherent drawbacks highlight the urgent need for alternative polysaccharide with distinct physicochemical advantages.

  Low-molecular-weight (LMW) polysaccharides exhibit advantages in constructing swallowing-adaptable macromolecular networks (Chen et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2023). With smaller hydrodynamic radius and higher polymer chain flexibility, LMW polysaccharides can effectively overcome thermodynamic incompatibility barriers, and deeply intercalate into gelatin matrices (Gentile, 2020). Abundant hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of LMW polysaccharides form tight entanglement with gelatin chains via non-covalent interactions, which avoids phase separation and promotes remarkable densification and reconstruction of hydrogel micro-networks (Zhang et al., 2024). The compact defect-free three-dimensional network can accurately tailor macroscopic rheological properties, and act as an ideal physical barrier against photothermal stress to protect encapsulated bioactive substances.

  Choy sum (Brassica rapa subsp. chinensis var. Parachinensis) is one of the major leafy vegetables in China, particularly in Guangdong Province (Zou et al., 2021). Tender stalks and flower buds are the main edible parts of Choy sum, with residual by-products exceeding 50% of total biomass (Artés-Hernández et al., 2023). Valorization of these by-products reduces resource waste and environmental pollution, facilitating circular agriculture. Previous studies have shown that the main bioactive components of Guangdong Choy sum include saponins, flavonoids, polysaccharides, alkaloids, among which the extracted polysaccharide (GCSP) exhibits excellent water solubility and biological activity (Hamed et al., 2026). In this study, GCSP was confirmed to be a LMW polysaccharide, showing great potential as a novel macromolecular regulator for gelatin network modification.

  Although composite protein–polysaccharide hydrogels have been widely explored, few systematic studies address how LMW polysaccharides induce microstructural densification through non-covalent crosslinking, and how such microstructural alterations correlate with swallowing rheology and bioactive stability. Herein, we fabricated LMW-GCSP/gelatin composite hydrogels via a cold-induced gelation strategy, and employed DLS, FTIR, and SEM to elucidate the microstructural densification mechanism. Furthermore, we systematically evaluated the suitability of these hydrogels as TMF matrices complying with IDDSI Level 4–5 standards, and verified whether their dense interpenetrating networks could act as efficient physical barriers to extend the photothermal half-life of encapsulated anthocyanins and achieve controllable in vitro release.


## Materials and methods

### Chemicals and materials
  Gelatin (CAS 9000-70-8) was purchased from Chron Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Phosphate buffer saline (PBS, pH 7.2–7.4，0.01 M) was supplied by Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. KBr (spectroscopy grade, CAS 7758-02-3) was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other reagents were of analytical grade without further purification before use.


### Extraction and purification of GCSP
  The extraction of GCSP was performed based on an ultrasound-assisted hot water method with minor modifications (Zhang et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025b). Briefly, the by-products of Guangdong Choy sum were dried, crushed, and passed through a 60-mesh sieve. The powder was dispersed in deionized water at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:30 (g/mL) and subjected to ultrasonic extraction (300 W) at 60 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation (Eppendorf 5810 R centrifuge, Hamburg, Germany, 8000 rpm, 15 min), the supernatant was collected, concentrated under vacuum, and precipitated overnight at 4 °C using four volumes of 95% (v/v) ethanol. The precipitate was reconstituted in deionized water and dialyzed against distilled water for 48 h to remove free sugars and small-molecule impurities. The purified GCSP was finally obtained via lyophilization.


### Structural characterization of GCSP

#### Monosaccharide composition
  The monosaccharide profile of GCSP was determined using High-Performance Anion-Exchange Chromatography with Pulsed Amperometric Detection (HPAEC-PAD, ThermoFisher, ICS5000). Briefly, 5 mg of the sample was hydrolyzed with 3 M trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) at 120 °C for 3 h. The hydrolysate was dissolved in 5 mL of ddH2O, and 50 μL of this solution was further diluted with 950 μL of ddH2O. After centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 5 min) and filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane, the supernatant was injected (25 μL) into the HPAEC-PAD system equipped with a Dionex CarboPac PA20 column (150 × 3.0 mm).


#### Molecular weight determination
  The molecular weight distribution of GCSP was analyzed using High-Performance Gel Permeation Chromatography (HPGPC, Thermo U3000, USA). The tandem columns BRT105-103-101 (Borui Saccharide, China) were used with isocratic flow rate of 0.7 mL/min of 0.2 M sodium chloride. The temperature of columns was set at 40 °C and the polysaccharide was detected on a refractive index detector (Shimadzu, Japan).


### Preparation of LMW-GCSP/Gelatin composite hydrogels
  A continuous blending and cold-induced gelation strategy was employed to fabricate the composite hydrogels. Gelatin powder was uniformly dissolved in deionized water at 60 °C under magnetic stirring for 1 h to prepare a 5% (w/v) stock solution. Subsequently, pre-dissolved GCSP solutions were added dropwise into the gelatin matrix to achieve final GCSP concentrations of 0%, 0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5%, and 2.0% (w/v). The mixtures were continuously stirred at 60 °C for 1 h to ensure homogeneous macromolecular complexation. To eliminate entrapped air, the mixtures were ultrasonically degassed for 10 min. The transparent or turbid precursors were then poured into specific molds and stored at 4 °C for 12–24 h to facilitate complete gelation network formation.


### Microstructural and intermolecular interaction analyses

#### FTIR spectroscopy
  The intermolecular interactions between GCSP and gelatin were investigated using an FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The lyophilized hydrogel samples were ground with KBr powder (1100 w/w) and pressed into pellets. Spectra were recorded in the wavenumber range of 4000 to 400 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 with 32 scans.


#### Dynamic light scattering (DLS)
  The particle size distribution of hydrogel was determined by using Zetasizer nano ZS (Malvern Instrument, Malvern, UK). Firstly, the hydrogel sample was prepared by dispersion to obtain a suitable suspension for analysis. Briefly, a small amount of freshly prepared gel precursor was dispersed in deionized water and diluted to 1.0 mg/mL to obtain a sufficiently dilute suspension for DLS analysis. The measurement was performed at a controlled temperature of 25 °C with a scattering angle of 173° (backscatter mode). The refractive index of the dispersant was set to 1.33.


#### Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis
  The internal microstructures of the hydrogels were observed via SEM (SU8010, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). To perfectly preserve the cross-linked pore structures without water tension-induced collapse, the hydrogels were pre-frozen at −80 °C and subsequently lyophilized. The dried cross-sections were sputter-coated with gold and examined under an accelerating voltage of 5–15 kV.


#### Rheological measurements
  All rheological experiments of hydrogel were performed using a DHR rheometer (TA Instruments, USA) with an aluminum parallel plate geometry (40 mm diameter, 1.00 mm gap). Silicone oil was applied to the periphery of the samples to prevent moisture evaporation during testing.

  An amplitude sweep was initially performed from 0.1% to 100% strain at a constant frequency of 1 Hz (20 °C) to determine the linear viscoelastic region (LVR). A strain of 1% was selected for all subsequent oscillatory tests. To monitor the sol-gel transition dynamics, a temperature sweep was conducted from 40 °C down to 5 °C, and then returned to 40 °C, at a controlled heating/cooling rate of 1 °C/min. Dynamic frequency sweeps were performed at 20 °C over an angular frequency range of 0.1 to 100 rad/s to evaluate the mechanical rigidity of the networks. The apparent viscosity was measured at 20 °C with shear rates ramping from 0.1 to 100 s−1. To simulate the structural breakdown during swallowing and subsequent recovery in the esophagus, a Three-Interval Thixotropy Test (3-ITT) was conducted. Phase I at a low shear rate (0.1 s−1, 60 s), Phase II at a high shear rate mimicking swallowing (100 s−1, 60 s), and Phase III returning to the low shear rate (0.1 s−1, 120 s).


#### Texture profile analysis (TPA) and IDDSI evaluation
  The macro-mechanical properties were analyzed using a Texture Analyzer (Stable Micro Systems Ltd., UK) equipped with a cylindrical probe (P/36R). A two-bite compression test was performed at a test speed of 1.0 mm/s with a target deformation of 30% (to simulate moderate oral processing) and a trigger force of 5 g. Parameters including hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, and resilience were extracted. Furthermore, based on the specific steady-shear viscosity and yield stress obtained from rheological data, the composite hydrogels were classified according to the International Dysphagia Diet Standardization Initiative (IDDSI) framework to verify their clinical suitability (Level 4/5) (Cichero et al., 2017).


### Encapsulation and photostabilization of anthocyanins
  To evaluate the protective effect of the densified network, 0.1% (w/v) blueberry anthocyanin extract was incorporated into the precursor solutions during the final cooling stage (45–50 °C) to avoid thermal degradation. The mixtures were stirred in the dark and cooled to form loaded hydrogels. For the photostability assay, uniform hydrogel samples (0.5 g each) were placed in a UV incubator under 365 nm irradiation. At predetermined intervals (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 days), the samples were withdrawn and thoroughly disrupted in 10 mL of pH 1.0 KCl-HCl buffer. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C in a dark shaking incubator for 1–2 h to completely melt the gelatin and release the anthocyanins. Following centrifugation, the absorbance of the uniform supernatant was measured at 520 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer, and the retention rate was calculated to assess the protective efficacy of the matrices (Lei et al., 2023).


### Statistical analysis
  The significance of the data was analyzed based on one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) at P < 0.05 level by SPSS Statistics 19.0 (IBM Co., Chicago, IL, USA). All data were presented as mean values with standard deviation (M ± SD).


## Results and discussions

### Extraction, purification and molecular structure analysis of GCSP

#### Low molecular weight characteristic confirmation of GCSP
  To obtain plant polysaccharides with intact structures and superior biological activity, ultrasound-assisted hot-water extraction coupled with ethanol precipitation was adopted to isolate GCSP from Choy sum powder (Fig. 1A). Moderate ultrasonic cavitation efficiently disrupts plant cell walls, markedly improving polysaccharides dissolution efficiency under mild thermal conditions (60 °C) and reducing irreversible thermal degradation of polysaccharide molecular chains. Following dialysis purification and freeze-drying, purified GCSP appeared as pale-yellow sponge-like solids.Fig. 1Extraction procedure and structural characterization of GCSP. (A) Schematic illustration of the extraction and purification process. (B) Calibration curve of molecular weight standard. (C) HPSEC chromatogram of GCSP showing the molecular weight distribution. (D) HPLC chromatograms of GCSP and mixed monosaccharide standards. (E) Molar percentage of monosaccharide composition.Fig. 1

  Molecular weight (Mw) is a fundamental intrinsic property of polysaccharides, dominating their hydrodynamic volume, molecular chain flexibility, and subsequent thermodynamic matching behavior with protein polymers in aqueous environments (Drusch et al., 2024). HPSEC analysis combined with a standard curve (LgMw = −0.211 t + 11.505, R2 = 0.9903, Fig. 1B) was applied to characterize the molecular weight distribution of GCSP. As shown in Fig. 1C, the elution curve of GCSP displayed symmetrical and narrow chromatographic peaks, reflecting high structural homogeneity and purity. The weight-average molecular weight of GCSP was determined to be 71.49 kDa, identifying it as a representative plant LMW polysaccharide. In contrast to HMW polysaccharides, which readily induce steric hindrance and macroscopic phase separation within gel matrices, LMW-GCSP possesses a compact hydrodynamic dimension. This structural feature confers GCSP with distinctive advantages in penetrating and uniformly intercalating into the three-dimensional gelatin network (Liu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025b).


#### Monosaccharide fingerprint
  To further elucidate the chemical mechanism underlying non-covalent intermolecular interactions between LMW-GCSP and gelatin, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was employed to accurately determine its monosaccharide composition (Fig. 1D). In comparison with mixed monosaccharide standards (Fig. 1E), GCSP displayed a characteristic fingerprint profile consisting of ten distinct monosaccharide constituents. Quantitative results revealed that glucose (Glc, 40.0%), galactose (Gal, 35.9%), and arabinose (Ara, 13.5%) constituted the predominant monosaccharides, accounting for 89.4% in total molar proportion. Minor constituents included xylose (Xyl, 2.3%), mannose (Man, 1.7%), rhamnose (Rha, 0.7%) and fucose (Fuc, 0.4%), alongside trace glucosamine (GlcN, 0.1%). The dominant Glc-Gal-Ara backbone structure exposes abundant hydroxyl groups (-OH) on GCSP molecular chains, serving as ideal donors and acceptors for intermolecular hydrogen bonding (Chen et al., 2026a). Notably, GCSP contained galacturonic acid (GalA, 4.9%) and glucuronic acid (GlcA, 0.5%), giving a total uronic acid content of 5.4% and confirming its nature as an acidic polysaccharide. In the gel system, ionized carboxylate groups (-COO−) from uronic acid residues provide negative surface charges for polysaccharide chains, thereby forming strong electrostatic interactions with protonated amino groups on gelatin polypeptide chains (Ma & Guo, 2026). In summary, GCSP was successfully extracted as a representative low-molecular-weight acidic polysaccharide with a complex monosaccharide composition rich in Glc, Gal, and Ara, along with uronic acid residues.


### Macroscopic phase behavior
  The fabrication and macroscopic morphological evolution of composite hydrogels are presented in Fig. 2A, alongside a schematic of the preparation procedure. In this work, a GCSP/gelatin composite system was constructed via a homogeneous blending and low-temperature induced (4 °C) gelation strategy. At 60 °C, aqueous GCSP solution was continuously added dropwise into molten gelatin matrix at a constant flow rate. This temperature, exceeds the helix–coil transition temperature of gelatin (typically 35–40 °C), keeping protein chains fully extended in a random coil conformation. Such conformational status facilitates deep infiltration of LMW-GCSP and sufficient intermolecular contact with gelatin polypeptide segments. Ultrasonic degassing eliminates internal microvoids, and concomitant mild cavitation facilitates homogeneous dispersion and intermolecular compatibility between the two macromolecular networks.Fig. 2Macroscopic phase behavior and non-covalent interaction mechanisms of the LMW-GCSP/gelatin composite hydrogels. (A) Schematic illustration of the preparation process of the composite hydrogels via blending and cold-induced gelation. (B) Macroscopic optical appearance (front and top views) of pure gelatin and composite hydrogels with varying GCSP concentrations (0.5%–2.0%, w/v). (C) FTIR spectra of the pure components and composite hydrogels. (D) Dynamic light scattering (DLS) particle size distribution profiles of the hydrogel precursors in the solution state with varying GCSP concentrations.   As shown in Fig. 2B, GCSP concentrations markedly regulates the macroscopic optical appearance of composite hydrogel. Pure gelatin hydrogel exhibits high optical transparency, indicating a homogeneous single-component cross-linked network with weak light scattering. As GCSP concentration rose from 0.5% to 2.0%, the hydrogels retained favorable self-supporting cylindrical morphology, yet underwent distinct optical transitions from transparent to milky white and highly opaque. Such pronounced turbidity variation is a typical signature of protein–polysaccharide complex coacervation.

  Driven by electrostatic interactions and intermolecular hydrogen bonding entanglement between GCSP and gelatin chains, abundant dense microphase-separated domains were generated, whose structural scale matches or exceeds the wavelength of visible light. Notably, unlike HMW polysaccharides that trigger severe macroscopic aqueous phase separation and structural collapse, LMW-GCSP did not induce destructive bulk phase delamination or damage to integral gel frameworks even after intense complex coacervation. This phenomenon directly highlights the distinctive superiority of LMW polysaccharides in preserving overall structural integrity and matrix coherence of composite hydrogels. Collectively, the macroscopic phase behavior demonstrates that GCSP induces progressive turbidity and complex coacervation without destructive phase separation, highlighting its superior compatibility with gelatin compared to conventional HMW polysaccharides.


### Non-covalent crosslinking mechanism
  To confirm the driving force underlying the aforementioned complex condensation phenomenon, FTIR spectroscopy was employed to characterize non-covalent interactions within the composite system (Fig. 2C). The FTIR spectrum of pure gelatin showed typical amide characteristic bands: a broad absorption peak near 3300 cm−1 corresponded to the Amide A band (attributed to N—H and O—H stretching vibrations) (Yong, Ahmad, Zhang, Gu and Zhu, 2025), a peak near 1630 cm−1 to the Amide I band (C  <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="20.666667pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 20.666667 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.019444,-0.019444)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg>   O stretching vibrations), and a peak near 1540 cm−1 to the Amide II band (N—H bending and C—N stretching vibrations). For GCSP, a broad absorption peak near 3410 cm−1 was observed, originating from hydroxyl group (—OH) stretching vibrations, while characteristic vibrations of C—O—C and C—O bonds in the glycoside structure were detected in the 1000–1100 cm−1 region.

  Comparative spectral analysis at varying GCSP concentrations revealed a significant dynamic evolution of the self-assembled system with increasing polysaccharide content. As GCSP concentration increased from 0.5% to 2.0%, the —OH and N—H resonance bands in the 3200–3500 cm−1 region underwent a significant and continuous low-frequency (red) shift. This observation indicates that with the increase in polysaccharide density, abundant hydroxyl groups on GCSP chains formed a dense intermolecular hydrogen bond network with gelatin polypeptide chains, which alters the vibrational frequency of intrinsic chemical bonds, and serves as the dominant driving force for initial molecular self-assembly (Wang et al., 2024).

  Meanwhile, the absorption intensity of gelatin-specific amide bands exhibited a non-linear fluctuation pattern, particularly during the transition from low to high GCSP concentrations. The slight shift and overlap between the Amide I band and GCSP carboxyl group characteristic peaks further suggest that, within the gelation microenvironment, negatively charged uronic acid residues (GalA and GlcA) on GCSP interacted with locally positively charged domains (protonated amino groups) on gelatin molecules. A subtle blue shift in the absorption peak corresponding to carboxyl group (—COO−) symmetric stretching vibrations in the 1400–1450 cm−1 region further confirms the charge neutralization effect between these oppositely charged groups, which induces gelatin molecular folding and aggregation, thereby constructing a more compact supramolecular structure. Additionally, the C—H stretching vibration peak near 2930 cm−1 slightly broadened with increasing GCSP concentration, reflecting hydrophobic interactions between the hydrophobic sugar rings of GCSP and hydrophobic amino acid residues of gelatin, which further compact the internal structure of the composite system.

  With the deepening of molecular self-assembly, profound changes occurred in the microstructure and spatial conformation of both GCSP and gelatin chains. The characteristic fingerprint peak of GCSP near 1078 cm−1 was significantly broadened with reduced vibrational intensity in the composite hydrogel. This phenomenon indicating that the GCSP chains of approximately 71.49 kDa were successfully anchored and embedded within the gelatin network under obvious spatial confinement effects. In the low-frequency region below 1000 cm−1, the vibrational peaks assigned to arabinose side chains were markedly weakened or even disappeared entirely. This finding suggests that these highly flexible side chains acted as binding anchors, entangled with gelatin molecules and immobilized within the composite network, which restricted their intrinsic conformational freedom.

  The broadening and weakening of gelatin's Amide III band in the 1230–1250 cm−1 region reveals that incorporated GCSP macromolecules impeded the self-association of gelatin polypeptide chains. This interaction partially disrupted the intrinsic triple-helix structure of gelatin and facilitated the formation of a more compact interpenetrating polymer network. Concurrently, the diminished intensity of the characteristic peak at 890 cm−1 reflects vibrations of β-glycosidic bond terminal carbons, indicating that extended GCSP backbone chains undergo adaptive folding and conformational rearrangement after interacting with gelatin. Synergistic hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions jointly construct the dual non-covalent crosslinking network, which tightly integrates discrete polymer chains into a compact and integrated supramolecular framework.

  Notably, when polysaccharide concentration reached 1.5%, the shift amplitude of major characteristic peak began to decrease, followed by a plateau at 2.0%. This concentration-dependent saturation behavior indicates that active binding sites on the gelatin molecules were gradually occupied, and the system transitioned from an initial rapid binding phase to a structurally stable state.

  To further validate the non-covalent interactions identified by FTIR spectroscopy, molecular docking was performed to visualize the atomic-level binding between GCSP and gelatin at the molecular level. As shown in Fig. S1A, the predicted binding conformation of the GCSP–gelatin complex revealed that GCSP preferentially binds to the surface grooves of gelatin. Detailed analysis of the intermolecular interactions (Fig. S1B) demonstrated that multiple hydrogen bonds are formed between GCSP and gelatin at various binding sites, indicating a tight and specific association. Notably, the arabinan side chains of GCSP contributed significantly to the binding through hydrogen bond formation with THR 135, SER 217, and SER 216 of gelatin (Fig. S1C), while the other side chains formed hydrogen bonds with HIS 105, TRP 107, and GLU 110 (Fig. S1D). These computational findings corroborate our FTIR results. The molecular docking thus provides direct visual evidence that complements the spectroscopic data, collectively confirming the formation of a densely crosslinked network through non-covalent interactions.

  In summary, GCSP and gelatin synergistically promote adaptive conformational rearrangement of molecular chains via diverse non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, hydrophobic forces and spatial entanglement, thereby constructing highly ordered and stable hierarchical biological composites.


### Evolution of gel precursor particle size
  To physically quantify the spatial structural rearrangement induced by the non-covalent interactions between GCSP, DLS was employed to characterize the hydrodynamic particle size distribution of the composite precursor in the pre-gelation solution state (Fig. 2D). The particle size of the pure gelatin system was mainly concentrated in the small size range, with the highest peak at 164.2 nm and a corresponding volume fraction of 14.1%. This size distribution corresponds to the typical random coil conformation or mild self-assembly state of single-component gelatin molecules in aqueous solution. The introduction of low concentrations of GCSP triggered significant electrostatic complexation and hydrogen bond entanglement between macromolecules, leading to a notable rightward shift of the particle size distribution curve toward larger sizes. For instance, when 1.0% GCSP was added to the gelatin gel system, the precursor exhibited a main peak at 295.3 nm with a volume fraction as high as 16.8%, which indicates the formation of larger primary complex aggregates in the system.

  Notably, further increases in GCSP concentration to 1.5% and 2.0% resulted in a significant elevation of the system's hydrodynamic particle size. Specifically, the 1.5% GCSP group retained secondary peaks in the hundred-nanometer range and displayed a prominent main peak corresponding to micron-sized condensates at 5560 nm with a volume fraction of 13.9%. The 2.0% GCSP group similarly exhibited a broad particle size distribution spanning from hundreds of nanometers to several micrometers, with its broad main peak distributed between 615 nm and 712 nm.

  This particle size evolution from the hundred-nanometer to the micron scale clearly confirms that LMW-GCSP acts as a highly efficient macromolecular crosslinking agent. It induces large-scale complexation and aggregation of gelatin polypeptide chains even before the solution cools and undergoes gelation. These micron-sized dense aggregates are subsequently frozen and fixed within the three-dimensional gel network at 4 °C, where they serve as solid crosslinking nodes. This process ultimately constructs a highly dense composite matrix with strong physical shielding capabilities.


### The densification evolution of the microstructure
  With the progression of molecular self-assembly, strong non-covalent interactions between GCSP and gelatin molecular chains drive a profound remodeling of the macroscopic physical structure of the composite system. This structural remodeling is most intuitively and quantitatively confirmed by SEM analysis. The baseline morphology of the single-component precursors clearly shows the initial state of the system from the morphology images of the pure components. As shown in Fig. 3, freeze-dried pure GCSP exhibited a relatively rough, dense, sheet-like or ribbon-like aggregate morphology, with no obvious porous network structure observed. This morphological feature reflects the highly self-entangled, dense, random state formed by highly flexible short chains following solvent removal.Fig. 3Microstructural densification of the LMW-GCSP/gelatin composite hydrogels. SEM images illustrating the internal morphological evolution of pure GCSP, pure gelatin (Gel), and the composite hydrogels incorporated with varying concentrations of GCSP (0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5%, and 2.0%, w/v).Fig. 3

  In contrast, the pure gelatin hydrogel (Gel group) displayed a typical single-protein network morphology: its microstructure consisted of a loose honeycomb-like network with large, unevenly distributed pores and extremely smooth, thin pore walls. Two-dimensional porosity calculations performed using ImageJ software on the SEM images revealed that the surface porosity of pure gelatin reached approximately 78.5%. The microscopic characteristics of this large pore and thin wall indicate that the physical cross-linking network formed solely by the spontaneous helices formation between gelatin polypeptide chains is fragile and contains substantial free water-holding space.

  However, the introduction of GCSP induced a dramatic and highly regular continuous transformation in the internal morphology of the composite system. A clear microscopic evolution was observed as the GCSP concentration increased from 0.5% to 2.0%. The originally loose and large gelatin pores were drastically compressed, with pore size reduced remarkably, and spatial distribution tending to be uniform. The thickness of the pore walls increased visibly, and the originally smooth pore wall surface became increasingly rough, even exhibiting obvious flocculent or filamentous attachments. This roughening of the pore walls serves as the most direct visual evidence of the physical deposition and deep entanglement of polysaccharide macromolecules on the surface of the protein matrix (Wang et al., 2023a).

  Notably, in the high concentration systems (1.5–2.0% GCSP), a large number of fine filamentous bridging structures appeared inside the pores, gradually forming a highly interpenetrating and extremely compact skeleton-filled three-dimensional network. Quantitative analysis via ImageJ further quantified this densification process: the porosity of the composite gel decreased to approximately 62.3%, 51.8%, 41.5%, and 36.2% at GCSP concentrations of 0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5%, and 2.0%, respectively. Comparative analysis of the transverse mechanical properties with other gel systems revealed that this polysaccharide-induced extreme densification phenomenon was consistent with morphological evolution observed in other food composite systems, while also highlighting the unique advantages of LMW-GCSP.

  In traditional sodium alginate/gelatin or carrageenan/gelatin systems, HMW anionic polysaccharides can reduce gel pore size through electrostatic attraction. However, their extremely strong chain rigidity and steric hindrance often lead to the formation of highly rough phase-separated aggregates at the microscopic level. These microscopic cracks can act as stress concentration points during gel fracture (Geonzon et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023). Conversely, the LMW-GCSP used in this study exhibited excellent compatibility, interweaving through the gelatin network like a micro-zipper. It forces conformational collapse and uniform recombination of polymer chains through high-density hydrogen bonds and electrostatic networks.

  When GCSP concentration reached 1.5% and above, the decrease in micro porosity plateaued, indicating that this non-covalently driven topological recombination had reached its thermodynamic limit, resulting in the formation of a highly ordered hierarchical bio-composite gel. The densification phenomenon observed in our GCSP/gelatin system is consistent with findings from recent studies on polysaccharide-protein composite hydrogels. Zhang et al. (2025a) and Zhang et al. (2025b) reported that blueberry leaf polysaccharide (BLP) likewise enhances the structural compactness of gelatin gels. Notably, at a BLP concentration of 1.5%, the composite gels exhibited a uniform and ordered network structure; however, further increasing the BLP concentration to 2.0%–2.5% disrupted the internal network architecture, leading to deteriorated gel behavior. This negative effect was attributed to saturation of cross-linking regions and aggregation of polymer groups, which compromised the cohesive structure of the system. In contrast, our GCSP/gelatin system maintained progressive densification up to 2.0% GCSP without structural collapse, achieving a more pronounced reduction in porosity. These comparisons further demonstrate that GCSP enables thermodynamically favorable network reorganization without the over-compaction drawbacks associated with certain other plant-derived polysaccharides. In summary, this crucial microstructural densification provides essential physical support for the dual application objectives proposed in this study.

  The macroscopic rheological properties of hydrogels serve as both a direct mechanical manifestation of micro-network densification, as evidenced by SEM observations, and a pivotal indicator determining their applicability as TMFs and carriers for active substances. #### Shear-thinning and thixotropic recovery
  Fig. 4A presents the apparent viscosity of all gel samples as a function of shear rate, with all systems exhibiting distinct non-Newtonian shear-thinning behavior. This phenomenon is generally attributed to the weakening of hydrogen bonds between polysaccharide hydroxyl groups and the -OH/-NH₂ groups on gelatin side chains under shear force. As the shear rate increases, polymer chains gradually relax, leading to network disentanglement and a corresponding decrease in apparent viscosity. Comparative studies have shown that the extent of shear-thinning is highly dependent on the charge characteristics of the incorporated polysaccharide (Yang et al., 2025a). Highly charged polysaccharides, such as carboxymethyl chitosan, exhibit higher apparent viscosity and stronger shear resistance than neutral polysaccharides at identical shear rates, owing to intensified electrostatic interactions with gelatin. This property is critical for swallowing dynamics: Under resting conditions or during oral preparation (at extremely low shear rate), high viscosity prevents premature flow of the food bolus into the pharynx under gravitational force, thereby mitigating the risk of aspiration. In contrast, during swallowing (when the shear rate increases sharply to 10–100 s−1), the viscosity decreases rapidly, acting as a lubricant for the food bolus to substantially reduce the muscular work required for swallowing and facilitate its smooth transit through the pharynx (Marconati & Ramaioli, 2020).

  More importantly, the thixotropic recovery property of the hydrogel is of great physiological significance (Fig. 4B, 3ITT). This test accurately mimics the real physiological sequence: resting (0–60 s), high shear during swallowing (60–120 s), and post-swallowing resting (120–400 s). Under high shear force, the temporary destruction of partial hydrogen bonds and non-covalent crosslinking sites within the gel network are temporarily disrupted, leading to a sharp reduction in viscosity. However, upon removal of shear force (after 120 s), the system viscosity undergoes rapid instantaneous recovery. Notably, composite systems supplemented with GCSP exhibit significantly higher recovery viscosity compared to pure gelatin. This secondary thixotropic recovery serves as a crucial anti-aspiration mechanism. It ensures that after the food bolus slides through the throat and enters the esophagus, the gel network can be promptly reconstructed to effectively prevent residue from adhering to the pharyngeal wall and being aspirated into the trachea (Wang et al., 2023b).

  To quantitatively evaluate the structural re-building capacity, the thixotropic recovery rate was calculated for each group. Pure gelatin exhibited a recovery rate of 57.9%, indicating relatively weak network reformation after shear disruption. Upon GCSP incorporation, the recovery rates increased substantially to 63.0%, 68.4%, 76.1%, and 70.9% for the 0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5%, and 2.0% GCSP groups, respectively. The 1.5% GCSP group displayed the highest recovery rate (76.1%), suggesting that moderate GCSP incorporation provides optimal non-covalent crosslinking density, sufficient to maintain network integrity yet flexible enough to allow rapid chain re-association after shear removal. The slight decrease observed at 2.0% GCSP (70.9%) may be attributed to excessive crosslinking, which partially hinders the mobility of polymer chains and delays their rearrangement. Notably, all GCSP-containing groups exhibited recovery rates exceeding 60%, which is clinically significant for dysphagia management, ensuring that the food bolus can rapidly re-establish its cohesive structure after pharyngeal transit.


#### Viscoelastic modulus and enhancement of food bolus cohesion
  Figs. 4C-4D further elaborate the elastic characteristics of the system from a dynamic mechanical standpoint. Across the entire frequency range, the storage modulus (G') of all samples was strictly greater than the loss modulus (G"), with no crossover between the two, confirming the formation of a genuine elastic gel network, rather than an entangled polymer solution. With increasing LMW-GCSP concentration, the G' of the composite hydrogel exhibits a significant stepwise elevation. Consistent with the constant shear stress growth curve presented in Fig. 4E, the composite system displayed a peak stress substantially higher than that of pure gelatin, indicative of enhanced yield stress. This finding is consistent with the microstructural densification (pore shrinkage and pore wall thickening) observed via SEM.

  Within the framework of the IDDSI, the enhanced G' and yield stress directly translate to superior bolus cohesiveness. This endows the hydrogels with sufficient physical resistance to preclude brittle fracture under pressure exerted by the tongue and palate, thereby preserving the integrity of the bolus volume during swallowing and closely complying with IDDSI Level 4 (extremely thickened) or Level 5 (finely textured) safety criteria (J. Chen, 2015).


#### Temperature sweep and Oral/intestinal response
  To further analyze the profound impacts of LMW-GCSP on the thermodynamic stability and conformational evolution of the gelatin network, we recorded the dynamic evolution of the storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G") of the composite system through temperature scanning tests (5 °C–40 °C) (Fig. 4F). A notable and counterintuitive rheological phenomenon was observed in the low-temperature plateau region (5 °C–15 °C): pure gelatin exhibited an extremely high elastic modulus, with G' on the order of 105 Pa. In contrast, the incorporation of LMW-GCSP (0.5%–2.0%) led to a significant reduction in the G' of composite gels by one to two orders of magnitude.

  From the perspective of traditional macromolecular gelation theory, this observation confirms the heterogeneous network recombination effect mediated by LMW-GCSP. At low temperatures, pure gelatin forms an extremely dense and rigid macroscopic network dominated by triple helices, which is sustained by highly continuous and undisturbed intramolecular hydrogen bonds. This excessive rigidity directly give rise to high hardness and brittle fracture defects exhibited in pure gelatin, posing a substantial ingestion risk. Upon the addition of GCSP, negatively charged flexible polysaccharide chains penetrate into the gelatin matrix and occupy partial hydrogen bond sites on gelatin peptide chains through re-condensation. This molecular-level spatial hindrance effectively inhibits the unlimited extension of gelatin macromolecular chains into a macroscopic continuous rigid network. Instead, numerous locally dense yet macroscopically relatively independent microgel phases centered on polysaccharides are formed. This also accounts for the observation that composite systems exhibit a denser microstructure while maintaining a more robust macroscopic structure, accompanied by reduced G' and hardness. This balance endows the hydrogels with excellent plastic deformation capacity and reduces the compression work required by tongue and palate for patients with dysphagia (Liu et al., 2026).

  In addition, during the heating process, all systems underwent a region of sharp G' decline, which corresponds to the unwinding and melting of the triple helix, and ultimately resulting in crossover with the G" curve. Analyzing the melting curve of the composite system revealed that, in contrast to the extremely slow and sluggish melting process of pure gelatin, systems supplemented with GCSP exhibited a more sensitive phase transition response in the range of 25 °C–35 °C. This change in thermodynamic properties holds clinical relevance for TMFs, with the system can undergo rapid gel-to-sol structural disintegration at near-physiological temperature (37 °C). This ensures that even if a small amount of food remains after sliding across the pharynx, it will rapidly liquefy under physiological temperature, thereby completely eliminating the risk of suffocation from airway obstruction by solid gel fragments (Stading, 2021). Furthermore, this thermal melting property at physiological temperature removes the physical barrier imposed by the dense network on bioactive substances. When the anthocyanin-loaded food bolus enters the gastrointestinal environment at 37 °C, the matrix spontaneously degrades, thereby achieving the burst or controlled release of natural antioxidants and enabling precise nutritional intervention.

  While our rheological and textural characterization has comprehensively addressed the bulk flow and deformation properties of the hydrogels, we acknowledge that tribological measurements—which reflect the lubrication behavior during the later stage of oral processing—would provide additional insights into the sensory perception and swallowing safety of the GCSP/gelatin composites. Due to the current unavailability of suitable tribometric equipment in our institution, such measurements could not be performed in this study. Nevertheless, our shear-thinning and thixotropic recovery results have partially addressed the flow-related aspects of lubrication, and future studies utilizing friction tests will be conducted to further validate the in-mouth lubrication performance of this promising TMF system.

  In the development of existing TMFs, researchers frequently combine gelatin with HMW polysaccharides (such as xanthan gum, highly branched starch, and carrageenan) to compensate for the insufficient mechanical strength of pure gelatin. However, these traditional systems are often accompanied by insurmountable rheological side effects. For example, while the xanthan gum/gelatin system significantly increases system viscosity, strong topological entanglement between ultra-long molecular chains imparts excessively high extensional viscosity to the gel, resulting in extremely poor stringiness. Stringiness causes food to adhere to the posterior pharyngeal wall and remain difficult to clear, posing a significant choking hazard during swallowing (Yang et al., 2026). In addition, although the starch/gelatin systems are cost-effective, starch is readily and rapidly hydrolyzed by salivary amylase in the oral cavity, leading to catastrophic viscosity collapse of the food bolus prior to swallowing (Oyinloye & Yoon, 2024).

  In stark contrast, the LMW-GCSP employed in this study imparts enhanced structural strength to the hydrogel while effectively avoiding the stringiness phenomenon, thanks to its shorter polymer chains that reduce topological entanglement. Furthermore, non-starch plant polysaccharides inherently possess resistance to salivary enzymes. This rheological profile collectively endow the GCSP/gelatin composite system with superior performance compared to traditional TMF matrices systems, making it a promising candidate for dysphagia nutrition intervention.


### Texture properties (TPA) and adaptability to IDDSI
  The fracture mode and bolus deformation capacity of hydrogels during oral processing directly determine their swallowing safety. TPA simulating tongue-palatine compression was performed to systematically evaluate the macroscopic mechanical responses of the composite system (Fig. 5).Fig. 5Texture profile analysis (TPA) of the LMW-GCSP/gelatin composite hydrogels. (A) Hardness distribution across different GCSP concentrations. (B) Calculated gumminess and chewiness values. (C) Springiness, cohesiveness, and resilience parameters. Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences (p < 0.05). (D) Adhesiveness evaluated via violin plots.Fig. 5

  Figs. 5A-5B illustrate the fracture mechanics characteristics of the system under compression. Pure gelatin exhibited high hardness (250.32 g), gumminess, and chewiness. In contrast, the introduction of high concentrations of GCSP (1.5% and 2.0%) led to a significant reduction in these three texture parameters. From a microscopic perspective, the high hardness of pure gelatin originates from its continuous and rigid macroscopic triple helix network. Upon the addition of GCSP, numerous polysaccharide-protein complex aggregate microdomains act as cross-linking nodes. As previously confirmed by DLS analysis, this microstructural feature densifies the micropores while disrupting the rigid skeleton of the continuous long-chain gelatin network, endowing the system with enhanced plasticity (Zhang et al., 2019). This moderate softening is of great clinical value in dysphagia management. Elderly dysphagia patients are often accompanied by severe tongue muscle weakness (Sarcopenia) and chewing function degeneration. The significant reduction in hardness and chewiness means that patients only need to exert minimal tongue-palate pressure to process of the food bolus, thereby greatly reduces eating-related fatigue (Suebsaen et al., 2019).

  The core and most challenging contradiction in TMF development is the requirement for foods to be sufficiently soft (easily crushed) yet not prone to disintegration (to prevent aspiration). Fig. 5C clearly demonstrates how the GCSP composite system resolves this contradiction. Although the hardness of the 1.5% and 2.0% GCSP systems decreased significantly, their cohesiveness remained at 0.85, comparable to that of pure gelatin. This is attributed to the high-density hydrogen bond-electrostatic crosslinking provided by the abundant free hydroxyl and carboxyl groups of GCSP. This microscopic molecular-level “zipper effect” firmly anchors deformed gel fragments, preventing macroscopic brittle fragmentation.

  Meanwhile, the springiness and resilience of the composite system were significantly reduced, which is a key clinical advantage. In the oral cavity, food with high elasticity and resilience (like rubber) are difficult to flatten. More importantly, they pose a fatal risk if aspirated into the airway, as they can fully occlude the trachea due to elastic expansion (a common hazard associated with jelly-like foods) (Raheem et al., 2021). The addition of GCSP transforms the gel from high elasticity to high plastic deformation. After being compressed by the tongue, the food can be easily flattened while maintaining its shape, creating optimal conditions for safe passage through the narrow pharynx. The violin diagram in Fig. 5D shows that the adhesiveness of the system increased with the addition of GCSP, decreasing from approximately −11.71 g·s to a range of −20 to −40 g·s. This is primarily due to the hydrophilic polysaccharide chains of GCSP, which expose more hydroxyl groups on the gel surface, increasing the surface tension of water with the probe (simulating the oral mucosa). Traditionally, excessive adhesiveness is considered problematic as it can cause food residue to adhere to the pharyngeal wall. However, for the composite hydrogel in this study, the cohesive force is significantly greater than the adhesive force, ensuring the food bolus tends to remain intact rather than adhering to and peeling off from the oral mucosa during processing.

  From a clinical perspective, the TPA profile of the GCSP/gelatin composites offers three distinct advantages for safe swallowing. First, the marked reduction in hardness lowers the compressive workload required of the tongue and palate, alleviating oral fatigue in patients with masticatory muscle weakness. Second, the decreased springiness and resilience signify a transition from elastic to plastic deformation; the gel flattens upon compression without rebound, thereby reducing the risk of complete tracheal occlusion should accidental aspiration occur. Third, the preserved cohesiveness ensures that the food bolus maintains its internal integrity, with cohesive forces overwhelmingly dominating adhesive forces—a critical attribute that prevents fragmentation and minimizes pharyngeal residue. This unique textural combination features softness, high cohesiveness, moderate moisture and suitable adhesiveness. It well conforms to the rheological and textural criteria for IDDSI Level 4 (pureed) and Level 5 (minced and moist) foods (Cichero et al., 2017).


### Anthocyanin photostabilization and IDDSI clinical swallowing safety assessment
  Based on the LMW-GCSP-mediated gelatin network densification mechanism and excellent macroscopic rheological properties, this study further evaluated the dual efficacy of this composite hydrogel as a physical shield for micronutrients and a safe matrix for dysphagia under real-world application scenario (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Validation of dual applications: anthocyanin photostabilization and IDDSI swallowing safety evaluation. (A) Retention rate of anthocyanins encapsulated in pure gelatin and Gel-1.0% GCSP composite hydrogel during a 10-day light exposure test. (B) Clinical simulations using the IDDSI framework: Fork pressure test and Spoon tilt test for pure gelatin and composite hydrogels with varying GCSP concentrations.Fig. 6

  Anthocyanins, as highly labile water-soluble polyphenols, are susceptible to ring-opening and degradation upon continuous exposure to ultraviolet and natural light. Fig. 6A presents the evolution of anthocyanin retention rates embedded in pure gelatin and composite gels (using the Gel-1.5% GCSP as an example) under light stress for up to 10 days. The pure gelatin system exhibited extremely limited protective capacity for anthocyanins, with the retention rate dropping sharply to approximately 23.56% after 10 days. This observation is consistent with previous SEM results, which confirmed that pure gelatin possesses a loose network with large pores and thin walls. This overly open three-dimensional structure is unable to effectively block the penetration of external photons and the free diffusion of oxygen free radicals or water molecules.

  In contrast, anthocyanins embedded in the Gel-1.5% GCSP composite hydrogel exhibited a significantly prolonged light half-life, with a retention rate still above 40.91% on day 10. Mechanistic analysis revealed that this significant improvement in photostabilization was directly attributed to the LMW-GCSP-induced microstructural densification. The substantial reduction in pore size and thickening of pore walls created a robust steric barrier and maze effect within the gel matrix. This dense physical blockade significantly restricted the thermal motion of anthocyanin molecules within the matrix, while effectively blocking pathways for external light energy transfer and degradation-promoting factors (Li et al., 2025). In a pectin-gelatin hydrogel carrier that forms nanoemulsions to protect pistachio peel anthocyanins (Taheri-Yeganeh et al., 2024), multiple pigment protection mechanisms greatly reduced degradation under continuous light exposure, and this system relies on nanoemulsions to achieve protection. In contrast, our approach achieves photostabilization purely through polysaccharide-induced network densification, which is a simpler and more food-compatible strategy. These results demonstrate that the composite system can serve as an excellent active ingredient reservoir, addressing the often-accompanied insufficient intake of antioxidant nutrients in dysphagia patients.

  To visually verify the safety of the system as TMFs, the fork pressure test and spoon tilt test, which are clinical benchmark assessments recommended by IDDSI, were performed (Fig. 6B). These tests simulate the fracture behavior of food when compressed by the tongue and palate during oral processing. When pressure was applied with a fork, pure gelatin underwent catastrophic brittle fracture, instantly breaking into multiple loose, hard fragments. In real-world swallowing dynamics, these low-plasticity fragments are highly likely to detach from the food bolus and prematurely enter the airway, potentially causing fatal aspiration pneumonia. In contrast, composite hydrogels supplemented with the GCSP (0.5%–2.0%) exhibits excellent plastic deformation capability. Upon fork compression, the gel flattened conformably and was squeezed through the fork tines, yet maintains a high degree of overall coherence (no fragments formation) after pressure removal. This finding perfectly aligns with the TPA data demonstrating low hardness and high cohesiveness, confirming that the hydrogen bond cross-linking network endows the system with superior bolus integrity.

  The spoon tilt test was used to evaluate the surface adhesion properties of the food bolus. Foods meeting IDDSI Level 4 or 5 standards should maintain their shape, while slide off easily with minimal residue when tilted. The composite system exhibited ideal moldability on the spoon and slid off completely under its own weight when tilted, leaving negligible residue on the spoon surface. This indicates that although the introduction of LMW-GCSP slightly increased the instrument adhesion value of the system in TPA, the extremely strong cohesion within the system under macroscopic wet conditions remained significantly greater than its interfacial adhesion to the metal or mucosa interface (Hadde et al., 2022). Collectively, these results validate the dual functionality of the GCSP/gelatin composite hydrogel: the dense interpenetrating network serves both as a safe swallowing matrix and as an effective physical barrier for anthocyanin photostabilization, offering a simple and food-compatible strategy for developing nutrient-fortified TMFs.


## Conclusions
  From an interdisciplinary perspective integrating macromolecular physical chemistry and clinical nutrition, this study successfully developed a novel dual-functional composite hydrogel based on the non-covalent cross-linking of LMW-GCSP (71.49 kDa) and gelatin. Departing from the conventional reliance on high-molecular-weight thickeners, this work provides a new molecular design paradigm that resolves the inherent trade-off between rheological safety and nutrient stabilization in texture-modified foods.

  The size advantage of LMW-GCSP improves the macroscopic phase separation defects typical of traditional polysaccharide-protein systems. With its small hydrodynamic radius and abundant hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic binding sites, GCSP acts as an efficient heterogeneous nucleation center, deeply intercalating into the gelatin matrix and driving thermodynamically favored topological reorganization of the micro-network. This transformation converts the loose, brittle macroporous structure of pure gelatin with 78.5% porosity into a highly interpenetrating, dense, thick-walled synergistic network (porosity for 36.2%).

  This microstructural densification macroscopically resolves the texture paradox in TMF development. The composite system disrupts the continuous rigid framework of pure gelatin, significantly reducing its hardness (from 250.3 g to 142.5 g at 1.5% GCSP) and elasticity to accommodate the declining oral and occlusal forces of elderly individuals. Meanwhile, it maintains high bolus cohesion (∼0.85) and secondary thixotropic recovery through dense intermolecular non-covalent interactions. This rheological reshaping, characterized by its plasticity, toughness, absence of stringiness, and resistance to breakage, can reduce the physical risks of airway obstruction and aspiration suffocation, complied with the IDDSI framework (level 4/5).

  Furthermore, the dense interpenetrating framework constructed via non-covalent crosslinks can provide a safe buffer for swallowing, and act as a physical shield for sensitive active molecules. Its labyrinth effect effectively impedes the intrusion of external light, heat, and oxygen, creating a photostabilized microenvironment that significantly extends anthocyanin retention (>40% after 10 day UV exposure) compared to pure gelatin (23.56%). This protective function is achieved purely through polysaccharide-induced network densification, offering a simpler and more food-compatible strategy.

  In summary, this study elucidates the molecular mechanism by which LMW polysaccharides reshape protein conformation and phase behavior. It also lays a solid theoretical foundation and broad translational prospects for the development of next-generation functional biomaterials, with high swallowing safety and precise nutritional enhancement for the ageing population.
